About this course

This is the foundational deep-learning course. It turns an introductory machine-learning background into working neural-network skill in PyTorch: framing a task in tensor terms, then building, training, and debugging networks. The emphasis is on building and experimentation, not on watching; every week ends in code the student has written, run, and can defend.

Modern AI rests on a shared deep-learning foundation, and this course provides that common base for continued specialization, including computer vision and large language models. It is lab- and project-based and designed for the way students actually work, with an AI coding assistant at hand, while keeping the learning genuine through a Build, Predict, and Explain model that grades understanding rather than authorship.

Course format. Thirteen weeks, each with three parts: a three-hour lecture (theory), a two-hour instructor-led practice lesson (live implementation and worked examples), and a weekly lab set as homework. The weeks form four parts: Foundations (weeks 1 to 3), Training Infrastructure (4 to 7), Architectures and Representation Learning (8 to 12), and Integration (13). A CNN mid-term mini-project consolidates Parts I and II; a final project consolidates the whole course. There are no written exams.
What you will build

Build, train, and debug neural networks in PyTorch end to end: framing tasks as tensors with a loss, then MLPs trained by backpropagation, data pipelines with Dataset and DataLoader, CNNs with normalization and residual connections, RNNs and LSTMs or GRUs for sequences, autoencoders and contrastive models for representation learning, and finally a fine-tuned pretrained model, delivered across eleven weekly labs, a CNN mid-term, and a final project defended in a short oral defense.

Expected outcomes

  • Frame a machine-learning task as a network: tensor inputs, model outputs, and a loss to minimize.
  • Represent images, text, and tabular data as correctly shaped tensors and manipulate them fluently.
  • Build data pipelines with Dataset and DataLoader, with clean splits and no leakage.
  • Use automatic differentiation (autograd) to compute gradients, and verify one by hand.
  • Implement, train, and debug neural networks in PyTorch with nn.Module.
  • Reason about optimization: SGD, momentum, Adam, learning rates, and schedules.
  • Diagnose training issues such as overfitting, data leakage, and optimization instability.
  • Build MLPs, CNNs, RNNs, LSTMs and GRUs, and autoencoders, and match the architecture to the task.
  • Apply representation learning and transfer learning, including fine-tuning a pretrained model.
  • Carry a transferable foundation into the advanced large-language-model and computer-vision courses.

Key topics

  • Tensors, autograd & the training loop
  • MLPs, CNNs & modern CNN design
  • RNNs, LSTMs/GRUs & sequence tasks
  • Representation learning & transfer learning

Theoretical foundations

The concepts and results this course rests on.

  • A neural network as a parametric function, and why a nonlinearity between linear layers is what gives depth its power
  • Backpropagation as the chain rule applied backward over a computational graph, automated by autograd
  • Overfitting, the bias-variance trade-off, and the train-minus-validation gap as the signal for generalization
  • Optimization theory: gradient descent, minibatch stochasticity, momentum, and adaptive methods
  • Convolution as local connectivity with weight sharing, and translation invariance from pooling
  • Normalization and residual learning, and why they make very deep networks trainable
  • Recurrence and backpropagation through time, and the vanishing and exploding gradient problem
  • Gating as a learned mechanism for preserving gradients across long sequences
  • Representation learning: bottlenecks, self-supervision, and contrastive objectives
  • Transfer learning: reusing pretrained features by feature extraction or fine-tuning

Prerequisites

This is a foundation course in the AI and Robotics area. It assumes a prior introductory machine-learning course together with the mathematics and programming background listed below. Readiness can be confirmed against the course's short refreshers and self-checks before Week 1.

Course-specific prerequisites:

  • Introduction to Machine Learning: regression, classification, loss versus metric, overfitting, regularization, and bias-variance
  • Mathematics: linear algebra, probability, multivariable calculus, gradients, and optimization
  • Python: comprehensions, generators, dunder methods, NumPy versus lists, and PyTorch idioms

Deep Learning Overview & ML-to-Network Framing

Framing any task as data, a model, a loss, and optimization; choosing the output layer and loss together; assembling the training loop.

Tensors & Data Representation

PracticeTensor creation, reshaping, and indexing in a notebook; broadcasting on several shape examples; a deliberate shape-mismatch error and its fix; encoding an image and a table onto the device.
LabEncode real data into correctly shaped tensors; predict whether pairs of shapes broadcast before running; explain the copy-versus-view and float-versus-long dtype rules.
NotebookWeek 2 practice notebook (Colab)
Wk 3
MLPs & Backpropagation
LectureThe multilayer perceptron and why nonlinearity makes depth worthwhile; nn.Module and the forward pass; the computational graph; backpropagation as the chain rule applied backward; autograd, .backward(), and why gradients must be zeroed each step.
PracticeBuild an MLP with nn.Module and train it; inspect .grad before and after zero_grad; check a hand-derived gradient against autograd on a tiny example.
LabBuild and train an MLP; predict what .grad holds after a second backward with no zero_grad; explain backpropagation and verify a gradient by hand.
NotebookWeek 3 practice notebook (Colab)
Part II: Training Infrastructure
Wk 4
Data Pipelines
LectureDataset (__len__, __getitem__) and DataLoader (batching, shuffling, worker processes); transforms; train, validation, and test splits; fitting normalization on the training split only; data leakage; batch size as a trade of gradient noise against speed.
PracticeWrite a custom Dataset and DataLoader and iterate batches; introduce a normalization leak, show the inflated metric, then fix it.
LabBuild a custom Dataset and DataLoader; predict whether a leaked-normalization score is higher or lower than the honest one; explain how leakage arises and how to prevent it.
NotebookWeek 4 practice notebook (Colab)
Wk 5
Loss Functions & Metrics
LectureTask-appropriate losses: cross-entropy on raw logits and class indices, BCE, and MSE or MAE; the loss versus the metric; accuracy under class imbalance and why precision, recall, F1, and the confusion matrix are needed; model.eval() and torch.no_grad() at evaluation.
PracticeRun a training loop with metric logging; show MSE on a classification task failing, then cross-entropy working; compute accuracy versus F1 on an imbalanced set.
LabWrite a clean train and evaluation loop with the right loss and metrics; predict an always-negative classifier's accuracy on imbalanced data; explain why the loss and the reported metric differ.
NotebookWeek 5 practice notebook (Colab)
Wk 6
Optimization
LectureBatch gradient descent versus SGD and its useful noise; momentum as an accumulated velocity; Adam's adaptive per-parameter rates; the learning rate as the single most important hyperparameter; step, cosine, and warmup schedules; reading the loss curve to diagnose.
PracticeTrain one model with SGD, momentum, and Adam and compare the curves; sweep three learning rates; add a step-decay schedule and show its effect.
LabTune an optimizer to a target; rank the too-small, good, and too-large learning-rate curves before running; explain what momentum adds over plain SGD.
NotebookWeek 6 practice notebook (Colab)
Wk 7
Regularization & Generalization
LectureOverfitting as a widening train-minus-validation gap; capacity and the bias-variance trade-off; weight decay (L2); dropout (on at train, off at eval); early stopping; and data augmentation applied to the training split only.
PracticeForce a model to overfit, then close the train and validation gap with dropout, weight decay, and augmentation.
LabDiagnose overfitting and attribute a generalization gain to a specific cause; predict the validation curve after adding dropout; explain why the gap, not the training loss, is the signal that matters.
NotebookWeek 7 practice notebook (Colab)
Part III: Architectures & Representation Learning
Wk 8
Convolutional Networks I
LectureWhy dense layers waste parameters on images; convolution as local connectivity with shared weights; stride, padding, and the output-size formula floor((in + 2p - k) / s) + 1; pooling and translation invariance; building a CNN classifier and tracking shapes and parameter counts.
PracticeBuild a small CNN, print the per-layer output shapes, verify parameter counts by hand, train on FashionMNIST, and visualize a few feature maps.
LabBuild and train a CNN image classifier; compute each layer's output size and parameter count by hand before checking against the summary; explain what a feature map represents.
NotebookWeek 8 practice notebook (Colab)
Wk 9
Convolutional Networks II
LectureBatch normalization and its differing train and eval statistics; layer normalization as the batch-independent alternative; the degradation problem in deep plain networks; the residual block out = F(x) + x that gives gradients a direct path; matching skip-path shapes with a 1x1 convolution.
PracticeAdd batch normalization and residual blocks and ablate each; show a deeper network training only with residuals; compare training curves, not just final accuracy.
LabAdd normalization and residuals and measure each with an ablation; predict which deep network trains; explain why batch norm behaves differently at evaluation. The CNN mid-term mini-project runs in this window.
NotebookWeek 9 practice notebook (Colab)
Wk 10
Recurrent Networks (RNNs)
LectureSequences and the need for memory; the RNN cell carrying a hidden state forward with weights shared across time; unrolling into a deep graph; backpropagation through time; vanishing and exploding gradients, and gradient clipping.
PracticeTrain a plain RNN, plot the gradient reaching the first step against sequence length to expose vanishing, and apply gradient clipping.
LabBuild a plain RNN and observe vanishing gradients directly; predict how the earliest-step gradient changes as sequences lengthen; explain why clipping tames explosion but not vanishing. The CNN mid-term mini-project concludes around this week.
NotebookWeek 10 practice notebook (Colab)
Wk 11
LSTMs, GRUs & Sequence Tasks
LectureThe LSTM cell state with input, forget, and output gates as a near-linear gradient path; the lighter GRU; sequence classification (many-to-one); sequence-to-sequence with an encoder and decoder; and teacher forcing.
PracticeSwap the RNN for an LSTM or GRU on the same task, inspect the gates and cell state, and compare long- versus short-sequence behavior.
LabBuild an LSTM or GRU and compare it to the plain RNN on an identical task; predict which holds a long-range signal better; explain the role of the cell state versus the hidden state.
NotebookWeek 11 practice notebook (Colab)
Wk 12
Representation Learning
LectureAutoencoders with an undercomplete bottleneck as learned compression, and denoising variants; self-supervised learning that creates its own labels; contrastive methods where the augmentation policy defines similarity; the linear probe as a measure of representation quality.
PracticeTrain an autoencoder, visualize reconstructions and a latent interpolation, and sketch a contrastive setup with its augmentation views.
LabTrain an autoencoder and probe its latent space; predict what a latent interpolation between two examples will look like before decoding; explain why the bottleneck must be undercomplete.
NotebookWeek 12 practice notebook (Colab)
Part IV: Integration
Wk 13
Integration & Transfer Learning
LectureTransfer learning: reusing pretrained features by feature extraction (frozen backbone, new head) versus fine-tuning with a smaller learning rate; matching input preprocessing; and the end-to-end workflow (data, model, train, evaluate, infer) that bridges into the advanced language and vision courses.

AI tools in this course.

Using an AI assistant is highly encouraged; it reflects how the work is really done. Two conditions keep the learning genuine: students keep full ownership of, and responsibility for, everything they submit, and must be able to explain and defend any part of it. Every weekly lab follows a three-part model. Part A, build: produce working code that meets a specification or reaches a target metric, using an AI assistant freely. Part B, predict and probe: before running anything, write down the expected outcome, then run controlled experiments and compare. Part C, explain and defend: explain why the solution works, where it would break, and what changed, ready to defend any line. Because the graded learning lives in Predict, Explain, and a short oral defense, understanding rather than authorship is what is assessed, and any AI use should be disclosed.

Student project

Two projects consolidate the course. A CNN mid-term mini-project (around weeks 9 to 10) is a convolutional-network project on an image task: build it, train it, run a controlled ablation, and report the findings. A final project (a one-page proposal around week 8, due week 13) takes a task end to end with any architecture family from the course, solo or in a pair: frame the task, build a suitable network, train and tune it, diagnose failures, and report results, then defend the design in a short oral defense. Grading weights the parts an AI assistant cannot do for the student: reasoning, interpretation, and defense.

Requirements

  • Build and train working networks, not disconnected snippets, and report results honestly on a held-out split.
  • For the mid-term, build a CNN on an image task and run a controlled ablation, with training curves and a clear statement of what it showed.
  • For the final, frame a task, choose a suitable architecture, train and tune it, diagnose failures, and evaluate on a clean test split.
  • Predict outcomes before running, then explain why the solution works and where it would break.
  • Submit a short report and a half-page reflection on AI use, and defend the design in a short oral defense.
