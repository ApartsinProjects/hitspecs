About this course

This elective combines Geographic Information Systems with the computational geometry primitives that make GIS work. Students learn GIS as an integrated system for capturing, storing, managing, querying, analyzing, visualizing, and communicating spatial information, and they learn the geometric representations and algorithms underneath it. Core GIS ideas such as maps, coordinate systems, projections, vector and raster layers, terrain, imagery, geodatabases, cartography, and web maps are taught alongside the geometry that implements them.

Every GIS topic is treated as both an application and a computation. A parcel layer becomes a set of polygons; a road network becomes a graph embedded in the plane; a terrain model becomes a triangulation or raster surface; an orthophoto becomes georeferenced imagery; and a spatial query becomes a geometric predicate, an index lookup, or a range search. The goal is fluency in GIS practice together with the computer-science foundations beneath it: robust geometric primitives, spatial predicates, planar subdivisions, triangulations, nearest-neighbor search, range queries, map overlay, spatial indexing, shortest paths, approximation, and scalable map processing.

Course format. Thirteen weeks, four contact hours each: a two-hour lecture (concepts and geometry) and a two-hour practice session at the keyboard. The course is project-based; teams carry one running GIS project end to end and present it three times, in weeks 5, 8, and 13. The weeks form three parts: GIS concepts as geometric objects, geometric algorithms for GIS analysis, and scalable GIS queries and map systems.
What you will build

Build one running GIS product end to end: real or realistic spatial layers modelled as points, lines, polygons, rasters, or a network graph, in a chosen coordinate reference system; a spatial predicate, overlay, triangulation, or spatial-index workflow that answers a clear GIS question; and a final map, query pipeline, prototype, or analysis artifact, evaluated for correctness, edge cases, and performance, and defended in three presentations.

Expected outcomes

  • Explain GIS as a system: spatial data capture, storage, management, querying, analysis, visualization, and decision support.
  • Represent GIS layers as geometric data: points, polylines, polygons, rasters, grids, and terrain surfaces.
  • Explain how scale, projections, coordinate systems, datums, and geodesy affect spatial computation.
  • Use geometric primitives, predicates, and orientation tests to reason about spatial objects.
  • Apply point-in-polygon tests and line-segment intersection to containment, crossing, and layer-cleaning tasks.
  • Use triangulation, Voronoi diagrams, Delaunay triangulation, and TIN models for proximity, terrain, and interpolation.
  • Describe map overlay, polygon clipping, and planar-subdivision operations behind layered GIS analysis.
  • Compare quadtrees, k-d trees, R-trees, and geodatabase indexes for spatial query workloads.
  • Analyze nearest-neighbor search, range queries, spatial joins, and routing queries in map data.
  • Use cartography, symbology, simplification, image pyramids, and tiled maps for scalable GIS visualization and communication.

Theoretical foundations

The concepts and results this course rests on.

  • Geometric primitives, orientation and sidedness tests, robust predicates, and degeneracies
  • Map scale, geodesy, datums, projections, and coordinate reference systems
  • Point-in-polygon tests, ray casting, winding number, and line-segment intersection
  • Convex hulls, bounding geometries, and monotone-chain or Graham-scan construction
  • Triangulations, TIN surfaces, Delaunay triangulation, and its Voronoi-diagram dual
  • Planar subdivisions, arrangements, map overlay, and polygon clipping
  • Spatial data structures: quadtrees, k-d trees, and R-trees, and their query tradeoffs
  • Nearest-neighbor search, range queries, and spatial joins over map data
  • Graphs embedded in the plane, shortest paths, and routing on spatial networks
  • Line and polygon simplification, scale-dependent detail, and geometric approximation

Prerequisites

This is a Year-3 elective. It assumes the mandatory CS core: data structures and algorithms, databases, and software engineering, together with the core mathematics (linear algebra, analytic geometry, and discrete mathematics). It additionally requires the specific prior background listed below; no prior GIS experience is assumed.

Course-specific prerequisites:

  • Data structures and algorithms
  • Programming, with Python recommended for the labs
  • Linear algebra and analytic geometry
  • Basic databases and SQL

AI tools in this course.

Using an AI assistant is encouraged for exploration, code review, data-cleaning ideas, map interpretation, and explanation practice. Two conditions keep the learning genuine: students keep full ownership of, and responsibility for, everything they submit, and must be able to explain and defend every GIS decision, geometric method, prompt-assisted result, and line of code. Every weekly increment follows a three-part model. Part A, explore: use an assistant to compare GIS workflows, generate small examples, clarify geometric predicates, or spot edge cases in spatial data. Part B, run and inspect: run the workflow, inspect maps and attributes, check coordinate systems, test edge cases, and confirm the generated output matches the spatial question. Part C, review and defend: explain the GIS meaning, the geometric representation, the algorithmic method, and where the result would fail or need better data. Because the course grades exactly what an assistant cannot do for a student, interpreting spatial results and defending decisions, every generated artifact is verified before it ships.

Student project

Teams of three or four carry one running GIS project from a spatial question to a defended result, presenting it three times across the term. Each team chooses a GIS problem, represents the data geometrically, and applies computational-geometry primitives or spatial data structures to solve it. The project includes a real or realistic spatial dataset, a clear GIS question, explicit geometric-representation choices, at least one core algorithmic method, and a final map, query workflow, prototype, or analysis artifact. Teams may use QGIS, PostGIS, GeoPandas, Shapely, CGAL, Python, JavaScript mapping libraries, or another approved toolchain; the point is not tool volume but the ability to explain the GIS meaning, the geometric model, the algorithmic method, and the limitations of the result.

Requirements

  • Build a coherent GIS product or analysis, not a set of disconnected exercises.
  • Use a real or realistic spatial dataset with an explicit coordinate reference system and stated data-quality risks.
  • Make geometric-representation choices explicit and apply at least one core computational-geometry primitive, algorithm, or spatial index.
  • Show real depth: evaluate correctness, edge cases, performance, and spatial interpretation rather than assuming them.
  • Work in a team of three or four and defend the design at each of the three presentations (weeks 5, 8, and 13).

Assessment & grading

Grading is project-based, with no written exam. Teams of three or four present one running GIS project three times, with weight on GIS understanding, correct spatial representation, algorithmic reasoning, map quality, validation, and the ability to defend decisions.
